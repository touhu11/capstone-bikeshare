Case Study: Cyclistic Bike-Share Analysis

An exploratory data analysis project investigating differences in bike usage between casual riders and annual members to identify opportunities for membership growth.

Introduction

Cyclistic is a Chicago-based bike-share company that provides bicycle transportation services to customers. Its bike-share program features more than 5,800 bicycles and 600 docking stations. The system offers traditional bicycles as well as three types of adaptive bicycles: reclining bikes, hand tricycles, and cargo bikes. These options provide greater accessibility for people with disabilities and riders who cannot use standard two-wheeled bicycles. Customers can unlock a bicycle at one station and return it to any other station within the system.

Cyclistic serves customers with different transportation and recreational preferences. Most riders prefer traditional bicycles, while approximately 8% use adaptive options. Most customers use the service for leisure, while approximately 30% use it for commuting to work.

To support its long-term growth strategy, the marketing director leads a team of six data analysts in developing promotional campaigns through email, social media, and other marketing channels.

After consulting with the finance analyst, the marketing director recognizes that increasing the number of annual members, also called subscribers, is important to Cyclistic's future growth. Rather than focusing exclusively on acquiring new customers, Lily Moreno believes that converting existing casual riders into annual members represents a promising opportunity. These customers are already familiar with Cyclistic and have chosen its services for their transportation needs.

Before the proposed promotional campaign can be presented to the executive team, the marketing director and data analytics team need data-driven insights to support their strategy.

As a data analyst, my responsibility is to investigate differences in how casual riders and annual members use Cyclistic bicycles and communicate the findings to the marketing director and executive stakeholders. The ultimate business objective is to identify opportunities to encourage casual riders to become annual members.

Stage 1: Ask

Business Question

How do annual members and casual riders use Cyclistic bikes differently?

Answering this question requires examining differences in riding behavior, including ride duration, usage patterns across days of the week, monthly trends, and the geographic distribution of starting stations. These findings will help identify potential opportunities for converting casual riders into annual members.

The analysis follows the six stages of the data analysis process: Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyze, Share, and Act.

Stage 2: Prepare

Historical bike-trip data are publicly available through the Cyclistic bike-share data repository . The data are provided in ZIP files and organized by year, covering the period from 2014 through 2022. For this analysis, the 12 most recent monthly datasets relevant to the business problem were downloaded and stored locally for further analysis.

The data are originally collected and maintained by Cyclistic. Relevant variables are included while protecting customer privacy and data accessibility. Records associated with staff service and system inspection are excluded to maintain the relevance of the analysis.

The datasets generally satisfy the ROCCC data-quality considerations: reliability, originality, comprehensiveness, currency, and citation. However, additional data cleaning is necessary to address potential false starts, incomplete trips, and instances in which users secure bicycles to docking stations without completing a typical ride.

Stage 3: Process

To maintain data integrity and accessibility, the original and cleaned datasets are stored separately in different local subfolders. The original CSV files are retained, while Excel versions are created for additional data inspection and transformation.

Using Excel sorting and filtering tools, I examined the datasets for inaccuracies, inconsistencies, and incomplete records. Trips with ride durations shorter than 60 seconds were removed because they could represent false starts or instances in which users unlocked and immediately relocked bicycles.

A new variable, ride_length_minutes, was created to calculate the duration of each trip in minutes. This variable is important because ride duration provides a basis for comparing the usage patterns of casual riders and annual members.

R programming was also used to sort, filter, and examine the datasets for inconsistencies, inaccuracies, and incomplete records. After the monthly datasets were cleaned, the 12 files were combined into a single dataset for the overall analysis.

The results obtained through Excel and R were compared to help verify the consistency of the data-cleaning process. This cross-checking provided an additional opportunity to identify potential errors before proceeding with the analysis.

Date and time formatting in Excel also helped calculate individual trip durations using the difference between trip start and end times. Records with inconsistent start and end times were removed to improve data quality.

Stage 6: Act

Based on the findings, I propose the following three recommendations to support Cyclistic's membership growth strategy.

  1. Develop targeted membership promotions. The monthly trend analysis indicates an opportunity to attract casual riders to annual membership. Cyclistic should consider targeted promotional campaigns that communicate the potential benefits of annual membership to existing casual customers.
  2. Leverage the existing station network. The geographic distributions of starting stations appear similar for casual riders and annual members. Therefore, Cyclistic can use its existing station network as a foundation for membership campaigns rather than assuming that different geographic locations require separate strategies.
  3. Promote membership benefits based on riding behavior. Casual riders have longer average ride durations than annual members. Cyclistic could use this difference to develop promotional messages emphasizing the potential value of annual membership for frequent or longer-duration riders. Actual financial savings should be evaluated using membership fees and trip pricing before making specific savings claims.

Conclusion

This analysis examined differences in bike usage between casual riders and annual members through geographic comparisons, ride-duration analysis, and monthly and daily usage patterns. The findings provide a starting point for understanding customer behavior and identifying opportunities to increase annual memberships.

By using these insights to develop targeted promotions and evaluate membership benefits, Cyclistic can make more informed marketingtouhu11.github.io/Project-Portfolio/ decisions and strengthen its long-term membership growth strategy.
